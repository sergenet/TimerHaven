How Long Should a Break Be?

Most people take breaks that are the wrong length, at the wrong moment, spent on the wrong thing. The research answer is more specific than you'd expect: 5 to 15 minutes, taken before you feel you need one, away from a screen.

A break is not a reward you earn after the work is finished. It is part of the work — the part where your brain files what you just learned, restores the attention you spent, and clears the metabolite build-up that makes the last twenty minutes of a session feel like wading through sand. Get the length right and an eight-hour day feels like six. Get it wrong and you finish the day tired and oddly unproductive, having "rested" for ninety minutes in total.

The short version. Working in 50-minute blocks with a 10-minute break between them is the most reliable pattern for knowledge work. If you use the Pomodoro Technique, the classic 25/5 rhythm is shorter but works for the same reason — the break arrives before fatigue, not after it. After three or four cycles, take a longer 15 to 30 minutes.

What the research actually says about break length

Break length is one of the few productivity questions with real evidence behind it, rather than self-help folklore. Three findings are worth knowing.

1. Micro-breaks help, and roughly ten minutes is the sweet spot

A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 22 studies on "micro-breaks" — short pauses of a few minutes to a quarter of an hour inside a working session. The result was consistent: micro-breaks improved vigour (how energetic you feel) and performance, and the benefit was largest on long tasks — exactly the kind that make up most knowledge work. The effect was strongest for breaks in the five-to-fifteen-minute range. Very short pauses of under a minute barely registered.

2. The most productive people rest in a 52/17 rhythm

The time-tracking company DeskTime analysed the habits of its most productive users and found a memorable pattern: the top 10% worked for about 52 minutes and then stepped away for about 17 minutes. That is not a magic ratio, but it sits comfortably inside the research range — slightly under an hour of focus, slightly under twenty minutes of rest.

3. Attention is a finite resource that refills away from the task

Attention Restoration Theory explains why a break works at all. Directed attention — the effortful kind you use to stay on a spreadsheet or a difficult paragraph — depletes like a muscle. It recovers when your attention is held effortlessly by something else: a window, a tree, a walk, a conversation with no agenda. A break spent scrolling does the opposite: it keeps directed attention switched on, just pointed at different content.

Why your breaks don't feel like breaks

If you take breaks and still end the day flattened, the length is probably not the problem. These three are, in order of how often they happen:

  • You take the break too late. Waiting until you are fried means spending the first half of the break recovering instead of restoring. A break taken at 80% capacity costs five minutes; a break taken at 10% costs the rest of the afternoon.
  • You spend it on a screen. Social feeds are engineered to capture attention, not release it. Fifteen minutes of scrolling can leave you more depleted than fifteen minutes of spreadsheet, because the content is deliberately stimulating.
  • You take breaks that are too long or too open-ended. Past roughly 30 minutes, the problem flips from fatigue to re-entry: you lose the thread of what you were doing, and starting again costs more focus than the break restored.

Break length by type of work

The right length depends on what you were doing. Use this as a starting point, then adjust using how you actually feel in the first two minutes of the next session.

Type of work Work block Break
Deep cognitive work — writing, coding, analysis, studying 50–90 min 10–15 min, plus a 20–30 min break every 3–4 hours
Meetings, email, admin, coordination 45–50 min 5–10 min; these tasks are less depleting but more fragmenting
Creative work — design, music, strategy 60–90 min 15 min, ideally walking: motion loosens stuck thinking
Physical or repetitive work 45–60 min 5–10 min of the opposite movement (sit if you stand, move if you sit)
Learning something new 30–45 min 10 min — consolidation happens during the pause, not the study

What to do with five to fifteen minutes

The best breaks share one property: they let your attention be captured rather than directed. None of these require willpower, which is precisely why they work.

  1. Look at something far away for 20 seconds. The 20-20-20 rule from optometry: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds. It releases the ciliary muscle that focusing on a screen keeps clenched.
  2. Walk, even indoors. A ten-minute walk raises energy more than a sugar hit, and the effect lasts far longer than the sugar does. Walking also tends to resolve the problem you were stuck on — several studies find solutions arriving during movement rather than at the desk.
  3. Drink water, away from the desk. Mild dehydration degrades attention before you feel thirsty; the walk to the kitchen is doing as much work as the water.
  4. Breathe out slowly for two minutes. A longer exhale than inhale nudges you toward the parasympathetic side of your nervous system. It is the fastest available way to come down from a stressful call before the next one.
  5. Talk to someone about anything else. A short, agenda-free conversation gives directed attention a genuine rest, which is why the "water cooler" break was never actually a waste of time.
The one rule worth keeping. If your break involves a feed, a notification list or a short video, it isn't a break. Your attention is still being directed — just by someone else's algorithm instead of your own task.

How to take breaks you don't have to think about

The reason breaks fail is rarely knowledge. It is that nothing in the day forces them to happen, and "I'll stop in a minute" is the most expensive sentence in knowledge work.

Put the decision outside your head. Set a countdown timer for the length of your work block and let the bell end the session for you — the point of a timer is that it removes the negotiation. If you prefer a rhythm with the breaks already built in, the Pomodoro Timer alternates 25 minutes of focus with 5 minutes of rest and gives you a longer break every fourth cycle. And if your problem is coming back too fast, a focus music track queued to start when the break ends is a surprisingly effective signal to re-enter.

If you want to know whether your breaks are working, don't guess. Track two numbers for a week with the Habit Tracker: how many breaks you actually took, and how you rated your focus in the last hour of the day. Most people find the correlation immediately — the days with three real breaks are the days that ended well.

Common questions

Is a 15-minute break too long?

No — for deep work it is close to ideal after a 50-to-90-minute block. Fifteen minutes is long enough to genuinely disengage and short enough to re-enter without losing the thread. The problem starts past roughly 30 minutes, where the cost of re-entry outweighs the recovery.

Should I time my breaks or just take them when I feel tired?

Time them. Feeling tired is a lagging signal: by the time you notice it, you have already been working below your best for a while. A timer takes breaks before fatigue, which is when they cost least.

Do breaks break your focus?

Bad breaks do. A break that keeps you on a screen, or one that runs so long you lose context, will cost you momentum. A short break spent moving, looking outside or talking to someone restores the attention you need for the next block — that is measurable, not anecdotal.

How many breaks should I take in a day?

For a focused eight-hour day, somewhere between six and nine short breaks is typical — roughly one per hour of work, with a longer one mid-morning or after lunch. If you are taking far fewer, the fix is usually a timer rather than more discipline.

The takeaway

A break is a fixed cost with a large return, and the length is the part you can control. Ten minutes after fifty is the pattern most people should start with. Move, look far away, leave the phone where it is. Then go back before the break turns into a new task.

Start tomorrow: one 50-minute block, one 10-minute break, on a timer. That is the whole experiment.
